What is a vaporetto?

Venice has no roads, so its buses float. A vaporetto is the city's scheduled public boat: part commuter service, part sightseeing route, and the only way most visitors will reach the lagoon islands.

Definition

A vaporetto is a public passenger boat operated by ACTV in Venice. It runs on fixed routes with fixed stops and fares, much like a city bus — only the street is the Grand Canal and the lagoon.

The word covers the whole public water-bus service, not a single model of boat. On any given route you might board a large double-ended vaporetto with 200 seats, a smaller motoscafo with a faster hull, or a low, open battello used on some island runs. What makes it a vaporetto in the everyday sense is that it is public, scheduled and paid for with an ACTV ticket.

That distinguishes it from almost every other boat on the water. Gondolas are private and rowed. Water taxis are private and motorised. Traghetti are gondola ferries that cross the Grand Canal at fixed points. Alilaguna boats look similar and use some of the same stops, but they are a separate airport service with separate tickets.

What the boats are actually like

Most vaporetti are functional rather than beautiful: a long cabin with rows of seats, standing room in the middle, and open-air benches at the stern. Windows are large, which makes the boat a moving viewing platform; doors are wide, which makes boarding quick at busy stops. There is no café, no commentary and no class system — just seats, handrails and a timetable.

Boats are busiest from mid-morning to early evening in high season, and quietest early in the morning and after dinner. If you are traveling with luggage, that difference matters more than any other piece of advice on this page.

01 — The network

A bus network with water in place of streets

The comparison to a city bus is not a metaphor. It is how the system is planned.

ACTV's water network is organized like a bus network. Routes are numbered, stops are named, timetables are published, and the same ticket is valid across the system for a set period. Transfers are free within the ticket's validity, which is why a 75-minute single can cover a journey that needs two boats.

The geography dictates the routes. The Grand Canal is the main artery, a four-kilometre S-bend that runs from the station and Piazzale Roma to San Marco. Line 1 crawls along it, stopping at almost every landing stage; Line 2 runs the same corridor with fewer stops. Beyond the canal, routes fan out to the Giudecca, the Lido, Murano, Burano, Torcello, Sant'Erasmo and the northern islands.

Because the city is dense and the water is shared with everything else, boats are not fast. A trip that looks short on the map can take forty minutes once you account for stops, no-wake zones and the traffic of gondolas, taxis and delivery barges. Build that into your day rather than treating the vaporetto as a taxi.

The one-line version: a vaporetto is to Venice what a bus is to any other city — cheap relative to a taxi, slow relative to walking, and indispensable once water is in the way.

03 — History

From steam launch to hybrid ferry

The name is older than the diesel engine, the ticket machine and the tourist crush.

Venice's first public steam launches began carrying passengers in 1881. They were small, coal-fired and named vaporetti — little steamers — to distinguish them from the rowed boats that had done all the work until then. The early routes followed the Grand Canal and connected the station with San Marco.

Over the next century the fleet motorised, the routes multiplied and the operator changed name, becoming ACTV in 1978. The boats grew larger and safer, the timetable grew denser, and the network took on the shape it has today. Through all of it, the old nickname survived.

Recent years have brought hybrid and electric propulsion, cleaner hulls and more accessible landings. The direction of travel is clear even if the pace is slow: the vaporetto is being redesigned for a city that wants fewer emissions and less wake damage, without losing the service that residents depend on.

04 — Use

Who actually rides it

Residents use the vaporetto the way people elsewhere use a commuter train: to get to work, school, the doctor, the market and the airport bus. It is not a tourist attraction that happens to accept locals. Peak-hour boats are full of people going about their day, which is why luggage, loud speakerphone calls and queue-jumping land badly.

Visitors use the same boats for a mixture of transport and sightseeing. The best-value version of that is simple: buy a travel pass, ride Line 1 end to end once, and then use the network like a local for everything else. The worst-value version is buying a single ticket every time you move, then discovering that three rides cost more than a day pass.

05 — FAQ

Quick questions

Is a vaporetto a ferry?

Functionally, yes: it is a scheduled passenger boat. But “ferry” usually implies a crossing between two points, while the vaporetto is a network of urban routes with many stops. In Venice, call it a water bus.

How many people fit on a vaporetto?

It depends on the vessel, but the largest boats carry around 200 passengers. At peak times they can feel fuller than that, especially on Line 1 and Line 2 between the station and San Marco.

Do locals pay the same fare as tourists?

No. Residents with a Venezia Unica card pay far less, and the city has a separate tariff for them. Visitors pay the published tourist fare, which is what the prices on this site refer to.
